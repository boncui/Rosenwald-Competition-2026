## TL;DR for pitching to them

- **He will want a catalyst and an edge in judgment, not in information.** "There's no information advantage anymore... there's only a judgment advantage" [6]. "It used to be about getting information. Then it became about judgement" [7]. Name the specific event (spin-off, merger, restructuring, activist campaign, asset sale, deleveraging) and show why the market is misjudging it.
- **Complexity is where he looks for value.** "Embrace complexity. Complexity is your friend... it is where you can add value" [6]. Complicated sum-of-the-parts, conglomerate, post-reorg and cross-border stories suit him better than a clean, widely followed large-cap growth name.
- **Lead with the downside.** He sizes positions by "what can go wrong" rather than by volatility [10]. He says "the losers are what kill you" and "the scariest positions are the ones you can't get out of" [6]. Show a valuation floor (sum-of-the-parts, asset value, deal price), a bear case with numbers, liquidity, and a clear exit trigger ("if the facts change and you wouldn't own it, you sell it... hope is not a strategy") [6].
- **Constructive activism and hidden assets appeal to him.** "Activism is number one in my book" [8]. His famous trades were sum-of-the-parts calls where the market ignored subsidiaries: Sears 1993 ("buying a $30 billion-a-year retailer for zero"), Philips' hidden TSMC/LG stakes, and Alcan's ignored units [5].
- **He likes going outside the US and into small and mid caps.** "If you really want to pick stocks, you want to pick them outside of the US... go to where the rocket scientists cannot go" [7]. He has also backed Japan conglomerate restructuring and European equities and credit [9][11]. York was a global platform (Europe, Asia, Israel) [2][12][15].
- **He is comfortable with credit and fixed income, especially stressed, distressed or bank-shed paper.** In his words, European credit was "the gift that keeps on giving" [11]. York's credit mandate: "good businesses with bad balance sheets" [48]. He has said "the more you move out the risk curve, the better the risk/reward is. Because the crowd is in the 'safe' stuff" [6]. A high-yield or special-situations bond pitch, or a credit ETF with a clear dislocation, fits his background.
- **Expect scrutiny of his personal sectors: energy/LNG, SPACs, leverage and binary outcomes.** York's 2018-2020 energy bet cost the credit fund heavily [18][20], and his largest public holding for years has been NextDecade, a hedged LNG developer [28][29]. (Inference) He will know energy and midstream in detail and will quickly punish a thesis that just says "oil goes up" or depends on financing. He avoids deals conditioned on financing, antitrust outcomes or a single decision-maker [6].
- **He will punish** a story with no catalyst, momentum or macro-only calls, positions too illiquid to exit, "hope" as the upside, and ignored disruption or regulatory risk (he has flagged Visa/Mastercard fees, healthcare, electricity and big tech regulation) [7].

## Investment philosophy & style

**Event-driven, fundamental, bottom-up, global.** York's stated principles include a global platform, cross-border collaboration, rigorous bottom-up research and constructive engagement with management [2][47][57].

*On edge:*
- "There's no information advantage anymore... there's only a judgment advantage." [6]
- "It used to be about getting information. Then it became about judgement." [7]
- "Embrace complexity. Complexity is your friend. For the simple reason that it is where you can add value." [6]

*On risk and sizing:*
- "The losers are what kill you." / "You don't have to dance with every girl at the party." [6]
- "Cockroach theory... When you see one, get out." [6]
- Start with a "non-full, 25 percent position" and add as risk falls. "If the facts change and you wouldn't own it, you sell it... hope is not a strategy." [6]
- "The scariest positions are the ones you can't get out of." [6]
- Liked asymmetric setups: "a couple of bucks down, but there's a lot of money up... you had a floor" (Medivation) [6].
- "The more you move out the risk curve, the better the risk/reward is. Because the crowd is in the 'safe' stuff." [6]
- He described never using leverage in merger arb [6].
- Judges risk by "what can go wrong," not backward-looking volatility, and limits position size by the downside [10].
- Regret from 2014: being too focused on drawdowns; wanted "more willingness to ride volatility" [11].
- On markets: "Uncertainty is not good. Certainty is good." [10]

*Deal-screening rules from merger arb* [6]:
- Avoid deals with antitrust risk, deals that hinge on one person's decision, and deals conditioned on financing.
- Target domicile matters (Delaware good; Ohio/Maryland takeover law bad).
- Do primary research, e.g. calling a local newspaper editor, or valuing Westin hotel by hotel and room by room.

*Sum-of-the-parts / hidden value* [5]:
- Sears 1993: "buying a $30 billion-a-year retailer for zero."
- Philips (hidden TSMC and LG stakes); Alcan's ignored businesses; Atlas Air, Advanced Medical Optics, Kansas City Southern.
- "Usually bets on stocks rather than against them" (Bloomberg's characterization).

*Activism:*
- "Activism is no. 1" way to create superior returns; "constructive activism... where there is a blueprint" [7][8][53].
- "We don't want to be a disrupter because we don't want to embarrass anyone"; works behind the scenes [8].

*Quality:* "You have to pay for quality" (on Israel/Psagot, 2005) [12].

*Growth / disruption:* "The big money will be made in the growth space." He flagged disruption risk to incumbents such as Visa/Mastercard's 2% fees, and regulatory disruption in healthcare, electricity and big tech [7].

## Implications for our pitch

1. **Frame it as an event with a date.** Name the catalyst, the timeline and what the market is misjudging. Say which variant perception is judgment-based rather than information-based [6][7].
2. **Put a floor under it.** Show sum-of-the-parts or asset value, net cash or the deal price, the downside in dollars and percent, a position-sizing rationale ("start at 25% of a full position"), and explicit sell triggers [6][10].
3. **Prefer complexity the crowd avoids.** Good candidates: a spin-off or post-reorg equity, a conglomerate discount, a non-US small/mid cap, or European/Japanese restructuring (inference, based on [5][7][9]).
4. **Consider a credit component.** Stressed-but-money-good bonds or a high-yield/loan ETF angle fits his "good businesses with bad balance sheets" lens [48][11]. The competition allows fixed income.
5. **Show liquidity.** He fears positions "you can't get out of" [6]. Include average daily volume or bond market depth.
6. **Energy pitches need a sharper edge.** (Inference) He knows LNG/midstream well and hedges energy beta [28]. A 2025 winning pitch was a tanker-rate ETF [1], so energy-adjacent ideas can win, but show the idiosyncratic driver, not a commodity call.
7. **Name the regulatory and disruption risks up front** (antitrust, reimbursement, fee disruption) [6][7].
8. **Mention a governance or activism angle if one exists.** A "blueprint" for value creation that management can act on resonates [7][8].
