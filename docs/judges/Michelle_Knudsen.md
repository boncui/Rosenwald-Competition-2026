## TL;DR for pitching to them

- **She allocates money to managers. She does not pick stocks.** She runs NYU's roughly $8B endowment, and outside managers do the investing [3][10][18]. She judges a pitch the way she judges a manager. She wants to know where the edge comes from, why the opportunity exists and why it should last, what role the position plays, and how it behaves in different market conditions [10][16].
- **Her hurdle is the long-term return of the stock market.** "The most reliable source of return over the long term is equity market exposure… Anything we invest in needs to be competing with that long-term equity market return" [10][14]. The pitch should say plainly why the idea should beat simply owning global equities, after costs.
- **Show the bad case and your plan for it.** Her career began at Goldman Sachs in 2008, during the financial crisis. She says: "You don't have to like the bad case scenario, but you do have to think about what it could look like and have at least the outline of a plan" [10][13]. She sizes positions so the whole portfolio can survive the bad case even when correlations rise toward 1 [10]. Stress tests, position sizing, stop-losses and "what we do if X happens" will land with her.
- **She loves process.** "A manager who can describe for me how they think about the world and how that translates into a portfolio is I'll listen to it all day" [10][16]. She also warns that "process doesn't necessarily yield returns" [10]. Treat backtests and track records as "the impetus to ask questions but not the basis on which we should make decisions" [10].
- **Quant, macro, relative value and trading-oriented strategies suit her.** She was "relatively early" to back hedge fund strategies that trade more and use more leverage. Her absolute-return bucket, targeted at about 25% of the portfolio, is "almost all" in those strategies [10]. The competition's quant/data and macro/fixed-income categories fit her background well.
- **Her current view of public markets:** passive investing, quant funds, retail traders and long-horizon holders have split the market, which creates room for nimble investors with a medium-term view. She thinks markets are "underappreciating the impact of private markets and of AI." She also says it is unclear "where the babies have been thrown out with the bathwater" after recent sell-offs [10][15]. She sees the end of zero interest rates as a change of regime, not temporary noise [10]. An idea mispriced by AI fears, with a clear catalyst, fits this lens.
- **Holdings (the 13F only shows a small slice):** NYU's 13F shows one position, the iShares Core MSCI Emerging Markets ETF (IEMG): $127.5M, 1,539,708 shares, as of 6/30/2026. It replaced a $3.2M position in EEM held at 3/31/2026 [28–30]. Almost all of the endowment sits with outside managers and does not appear on the 13F.
- **She was a judge in 2025 too** [26]. Expect tough questions from an institutional point of view: leverage, sizing, liquidity, fees, and whether the structure matches the opportunity [10][11].

## Investment philosophy & style (quotes cited)

**1. Equity returns are the benchmark for everything else.**
> "The most reliable source of return over the long term is equity market exposure and if we are going to generate a return that is going to fulfill the university's objectives of spend plus preserving purchasing power. Anything we invest in needs to be competing with that long-term equity market return. And there are lots of reasons to move away from equity market returns, but we've got to be really clear on what those reasons are for each part of the portfolio." [10] (~28:25 [14])

**2. Plan for the downside first, rooted in her 2008 start.**
> "I think the biggest thing is you always have to be prepared for the bad case scenario. You don't have to like the bad case scenario, but you do have to think about what it could look like and have at least the outline of a plan for what you'll do in that situation." [10][13]
> "When we think about sizing these higher octane hedge fund strategies in the portfolio, we size the total portfolio so that if we get that bad case scenario across all of our managers at the same time, we can live with it." [10]
> "I think it's really hard to understand the downside until you're staring at the numbers on a piece of paper." [10]

**3. Why does the opportunity exist, and why should it last?**
> "Really trying to pressure test for ourselves why a certain opportunity set exists and how we think that opportunity set is best accessed… everything from why active management in this market versus passive to why does this royalty stream exist… why is there a return here and why should it persist." [10]
> She also looks for mismatches "between an opportunity set, maybe the duration of that opportunity set, and the structure that a manager is offering." [10]

**4. Data first, but looking forward.**
> "We're very data driven and that's across every single asset class… We mix that with a very forward-looking perspective." [10][16]
> "Any backwards looking data… should be the impetus to ask questions but not the basis on which we should make decisions." [10]

**5. Process, edge and role in the portfolio.**
> "I love process… a manager who can describe for me how they think about the world and how that translates into a portfolio is I'll listen to it all day. I think the thing I've learned over time though is process doesn't necessarily yield returns." [10][16]
> "It really came down to identifying the edge that a manager had and then focusing on the role that they play in the portfolio." [10]

**6. Returns after fees and taxes.** At an OCIO or asset manager "you have no right to exist," so everything is judged after fees. She also stresses that "net of tax investing" is "a whole another ballgame" [10][13].

**7. Good decisions and good outcomes are different things.** She repeats her father's line: "a good outcome doesn't mean you made a good decision." She adds that investors must keep working after a decision, because exiting, not re-upping or re-upping is as important as the first decision [10].

**8. Culture.** Her pet peeve is "moaning or complaining without taking responsibility for fixing the problem." She wants people to say "here's a problem. Here's what I think we should do about it" [10]. She values "continuous improvement," hustle and debate in the room [10].

## Implications for our pitch

*All of the following are inferences drawn from the evidence above.*

1. **Open with the opportunity set.** Say why the mispricing exists (forced sellers, index or passive flows, a quant or retail crowding effect, a complicated structure, AI fear) and why it should persist long enough to pay. This mirrors her main diligence question [10].
2. **Measure the idea against global equities.** Show expected return against an ACWI/S&P-type hurdle over the holding period, after ETF fees, FX hedging costs and dividend withholding. That is her hurdle [10], and the competition scores in USD [26].
3. **Include a real bad-case section.** Give the scenario, the probability-weighted loss, a correlation-stress view (what if everything sells off together), position sizing, and a stop-loss or exit plan. Explain what you would do, not only what could go wrong [10].
4. **Present the process as a repeatable framework**, from data to thesis to position, with a clear role in a portfolio. Show a backtest as a question-raiser, with checks that it holds up out of sample, not as proof [10].
5. **Angles likely to resonate:** a quant/systematic or cross-asset macro idea with a clean explanation of where the return comes from; a medium-term mispricing tied to AI disruption, oversold or not yet priced in; an emerging-markets idea (NYU's only visible direct position is EM beta [28]); relative-value logic expressed long-only through ETFs (for example, a long ETF with an explicit hedge rationale). Stay within the no-shorts, no-derivatives rules and address inverse-ETF risks if you use them [26].
6. **Expect institutional questions:** Is there embedded leverage (leveraged or inverse ETFs, highly levered issuers)? How liquid is the position and what size would it take? Does the structure match the opportunity? Answer succinctly. She rewards over-preparation and "here's the problem, here's what we'd do" [10].
7. **Avoid storytelling with no numbers, and avoid a single bullish case with no downside plan.** For oil and gas extraction ideas, note that NYU's own investment policy excludes them. Argue on merit and don't dwell on it [33].
